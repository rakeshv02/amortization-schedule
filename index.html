How an Amortization Schedule Works

An amortization schedule breaks every loan payment into two parts: interest charged on the current balance, and principal that reduces what you owe. Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is high; as the balance falls, more of each payment goes to principal. The monthly payment stays the same, but its split shifts over the term.

The fixed payment is found with the standard formula M = P × [ r(1+r)^n ] ÷ [ (1+r)^n − 1 ], where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of payments. For example, a £200,000 loan at 4.5% over 25 years works out to roughly £1,112 per month.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an amortization schedule?

An amortization schedule is a table showing each loan payment over the full term, split into interest and principal, along with the remaining balance after every payment. It lets you see exactly how a loan is paid down month by month. This tool builds the schedule automatically from your loan amount, rate, and term.

Why is most of my early payment going to interest?

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is at its highest at the start of the loan, so early payments are mostly interest. As the principal shrinks, the interest portion falls and more of each fixed payment reduces the balance. This is why extra payments early on save the most interest.

How is the monthly loan payment calculated?

The fixed monthly payment uses the standard amortization formula based on the loan amount, the monthly interest rate, and the total number of payments. It is set so the balance reaches exactly zero on the final payment. Changing the rate or term recalculates the payment instantly above.

Does making extra payments change the schedule?

Yes. Any extra payment goes straight to principal, which lowers future interest and shortens the loan term. Because interest is front-loaded, extra payments made early have the biggest impact on total interest saved. Check with your lender that overpayments are allowed without penalty.

How to Use the Amortization Schedule

An amortization schedule breaks down every single loan payment into its principal and interest components, showing you exactly how your debt reduces over time. Whether you have a home mortgage, car loan, or personal loan, this tool generates a complete payment table from your first repayment to your last.

To use the calculator, enter your loan amount (principal), the annual interest rate, and the loan term in years or months. The tool instantly calculates your fixed monthly payment and produces a line-by-line schedule showing how much of each payment goes toward interest versus reducing your balance.

A key nuance of amortization is that early payments are predominantly interest while later payments are mostly principal. On a typical 25-year mortgage, you may pay more in interest in the first year than you reduce the principal. Understanding this is critical for making overpayment decisions or evaluating refinancing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is amortization?

Amortization is the process of paying off a loan through regular scheduled payments over time. Each payment covers accrued interest first, with the remainder reducing the outstanding principal balance.

Why do early payments go mostly to interest?

Interest is calculated on the remaining balance. Because the balance is highest at the start of the loan, the interest portion of each payment is also highest. As you pay down the balance, the interest portion shrinks and more of each payment goes to principal.

Can I use this for any type of loan?

Yes. The amortization schedule works for mortgages, car loans, personal loans, student loans, or any fixed-rate loan with equal monthly payments. Simply enter the correct principal, rate, and term.

How much can I save by making overpayments?

Even small regular overpayments can save significant interest. On a £200,000 mortgage at 5% over 25 years, paying an extra £100/month saves approximately £22,000 in interest and cuts the term by nearly 3 years.

What happens if my interest rate changes?

A standard amortization schedule assumes a fixed rate throughout. If you have a variable-rate loan, you would need to recalculate the schedule each time the rate changes, treating the remaining balance as a new loan.
